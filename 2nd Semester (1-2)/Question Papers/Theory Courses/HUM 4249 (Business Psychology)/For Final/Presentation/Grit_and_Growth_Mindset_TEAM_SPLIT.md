# Beyond Talent: How Grit and Growth Mindset Shape Success

## 🎤 MUSADDIK — Group Introduction + Opening Hook
**(~1.5 min)**

Assalamualaikum, everyone.

Today we want to talk about a question most of us have quietly asked ourselves at some point in university: *why do some people seem to get better at hard things, while others stay stuck?*

I'm Musaddik, and this is my team — Jahin, Alhadi Baba, Waseef Munir, Fahad, and Mugdho. Together, we're going to walk you through two ideas that, we think, explain that question better than "talent" ever does.

So let's start with a story.

Imagine two students starting university on the same day.

Student A and Student B.

Same intelligence. Same work ethic. Same grades in their first semester. If you looked at their transcripts, you couldn't tell them apart.

Then, in the second semester, both of them hit something hard. A brutal programming course. A math class that stops making sense. A research project where nothing works.

Student A studies, tries, fails. And a thought creeps in:

*"Maybe I'm just not good at this."*

That thought changes everything. The difficulty stops being a difficult problem — it becomes evidence about who Student A *is*.

Student B fails too. Same grade. Same mistakes. Same frustration.

But Student B thinks:

*"I'm not good at this... yet."*

One word. And that word changes the entire story.

Student B asks a friend for help. Reviews the mistakes. Tries again. Fails again. Keeps going.

Fast-forward six months. Same starting point, same opportunities — so why might these two people be in completely different places?

That's the question behind our presentation today:

**Is success mostly about how talented you are when you start — or about what you do when things get hard?**

## 🎤 ALHADI BABA — The Talent Myth + Introducing Duckworth
**(~1.75 min)**

We love a simple story about success.

We see someone great at programming and say: *"He's just naturally talented."*
We see someone acing every exam and say: *"She's just really smart."*
We see a phenomenal athlete and say: *"Born with it."*

And talent is real. People start from different places, with different resources and different opportunities. We're not pretending that's not true.

But talent doesn't explain everything. And that's exactly what psychologist **Angela Duckworth** noticed — not in a lab, but in a classroom.

She taught seventh-grade math in New York City. And she kept seeing something that didn't add up: her strongest performers weren't always her highest-IQ students. Some of her most obviously intelligent kids weren't the ones performing best.

That gap bothered her enough to change her career. She left teaching and became a psychologist, asking one question:

*If intelligence doesn't fully explain achievement — what does?*

Instead of studying one kind of successful person, she studied wildly different ones: West Point military cadets surviving brutal training. Kids competing in the National Spelling Bee. Teachers thriving in under-resourced, high-pressure schools. Rookie salespeople trying to survive their first year.

Completely different worlds. Same underlying question in every single one: **who keeps going, and who quits?**

One trait kept showing up, again and again, in the people who kept going — regardless of the field.

She called it **grit**. And it became the subject of her bestselling book, *Grit: The Power of Passion and Perseverance.*

## 🎤 WASEEF MUNIR — What Grit Actually Is
**(~1.75 min)**

So let's be precise about what grit actually is — because it's often misunderstood, and honestly, oversimplified.

Grit is *not* working hard for one day.
It's *not* constant motivation.
It's *not* pretending failure doesn't hurt.

Duckworth defines it as **passion and perseverance toward long-term goals** — the ability to keep pursuing something that matters, over years, even when the process gets boring, slow, or genuinely painful.

Here's a simple way to separate motivation from grit. Motivation is what you feel on a Sunday night, writing an ambitious study plan, promising yourself six hours of focused work a day. Grit is what's left three weeks later — when the excitement is gone, the material is boring, you just failed a quiz anyway, and nobody's watching to see if you keep going.

Grit asks one blunt question: **can you keep going after the motivation has completely disappeared?**

Duckworth has a metaphor for this that captures it perfectly:

> *"Life is a marathon, not a sprint."*

A sprint rewards speed, for a short burst. A marathon rewards something else entirely — the ability to stay in it, mile after mile, long after the adrenaline is gone.

Nobody becomes a strong programmer by solving problems for one intense night. Nobody becomes a great musician from a single five-hour practice session. You get there by showing up again. And again. Long after it stopped being exciting — and long before anyone's applauding you for it.

## 🎤 FAHAD — Where Grit Comes From + Fixed vs. Growth Mindset
**(~1.75 min)**

So here's a reasonable question: if grit matters this much, can we just tell someone, *"be more persistent"*?

Duckworth's own answer is refreshingly honest: **no.** There's no simple formula — she says so herself, in her own research. Psychology isn't a motivational poster. "Never give up" is not, on its own, a strategy anyone can actually use.

But she does point toward one of the most promising answers we have on this — and it comes from a different psychologist entirely: **Carol Dweck**, and her concept of the **growth mindset**, from her influential book, *Mindset: The New Psychology of Success.*

**[SLIDE CUE: show *Mindset* book cover here]**

Let's go back to Student A and Student B one more time.

Same failure. Two completely different internal stories.

*"I'm bad at this."* versus *"I'm not good at this yet."*

That gap — that one word — is what Dweck calls **mindset**.

A **fixed mindset** treats ability as basically set in stone. You're either good at math, or you're not. Either "a programming person," or you're not. Under a fixed mindset, failure isn't just a bad result — it becomes a verdict on your identity.

A **growth mindset** treats ability as something that develops — through practice, strategy, feedback, and time. Under a growth mindset, failure stops meaning *"I can't."* It starts meaning *"I haven't learned this yet."*

## 🎤 MUGDHO — Practical Takeaways
**(~1.75 min)**

And that single shift changes everything downstream. A hard problem becomes less threatening. A mistake becomes data instead of a judgment. Feedback becomes useful instead of painful. Effort starts to actually mean something.

So, knowing all this — what does it actually mean for us, practically, as university students starting exactly where Student A and Student B started?

Four small shifts, starting today:

**One** — change the internal sentence. Swap *"I can't do this"* for *"I can't do this yet."*

**Two** — change the question you ask after failure. Not *"why am I bad at this,"* but *"what did this failure just teach me?"*

**Three** — don't confuse effort with improvement. If a strategy isn't working, the answer isn't more of the same thing — it's feedback, a different approach, or someone who's already solved the problem you're stuck on.

**Four** — choose something actually worth the discomfort. Grit isn't suffering for its own sake. It's persistence *in service of something that genuinely matters to you.*

But before we wrap up — there's one honest caveat our team wants to leave you with. Because it would be easy to turn all of this into a motivational cliché, and that's not what good psychology does.

## 🎤 JAHIN — Where the Two Ideas Meet + The Honest Caveat
**(~2.25 min)**

Here's where *Grit* and *Mindset* actually connect — and it's worth being precise, because they are genuinely not the same thing.

**Growth mindset** is a belief: *"I can get better at this."*
**Grit** is a behavior: *"I will keep working at it — even now, even today."*

Belief alone doesn't get you through week six of slow, frustrating, invisible progress. You can genuinely believe improvement is possible and still quit. That's exactly where grit takes over — it's the discipline that keeps showing up *after* the mindset shift has already happened.

Together, these two ideas build a cycle:

**Challenge → Failure → Reflection → Learning → Improvement → Confidence → Bigger challenge — and repeat.**

Run that loop enough times, and something interesting happens: the person who wasn't very good at something slowly becomes very good at it. And when we meet them at the *end* of that loop, we call them "talented." We don't see the hundreds of failed attempts behind it. We just see the highlight reel — and we mistake it for talent.

But grit and growth mindset are **not a guarantee.** A gritty person can still fail. Someone can work incredibly hard and still fall short — because success depends on more than internal drive. It depends on opportunity, resources, education, support systems, and circumstances that are sometimes completely outside anyone's control. Telling someone from a genuinely disadvantaged situation that they "just need more grit" isn't insight — it can be a way of ignoring the real barriers actually in front of them.

There's one more nuance worth naming clearly: **persistence is not the same as repeating a failing strategy, just harder.** Grinding through the same broken method isn't grit — it's just stubbornness wearing grit's clothing. Real perseverance sometimes means changing the approach, asking for help, or getting honest feedback — not gripping the original plan tighter and hoping.

So grit doesn't mean *"never change your plan."* It means *"don't give up on meaningful progress just because your first attempt failed."*

## 🎤 MUSADDIK — Closing
**(~1.5 min)**

So — let's go back to where we started. Two students. Same ability. Same opportunities. Same first semester.

Then difficulty arrives — and they diverge.

One treats failure as proof of a ceiling. The other treats failure as part of the process.

Talent decided where they *started*. It did not decide where they *ended up*.

Growth mindset changed how they interpreted the struggle. Grit is what kept them in the race long enough for that mindset to actually matter.

Neither one works alone. Both need effort, feedback, the right environment — and sometimes, honestly, a bit of luck.

So maybe the real question isn't *"Am I talented enough to succeed?"*

Maybe it's:

*"Am I willing to learn? Am I willing to adapt? And when something actually matters to me — am I willing to keep going?"*

Because very often, the person who looks talented at the finish line was simply the person who kept learning long enough for their ability to catch up to their goal.

Talent gives you a starting line.
Growth mindset tells you the finish line can move.
Grit is what gets you there anyway.

Thank you.
