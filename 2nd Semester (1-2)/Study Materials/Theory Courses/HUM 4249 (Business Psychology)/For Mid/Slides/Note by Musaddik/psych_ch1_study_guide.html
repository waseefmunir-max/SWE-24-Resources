01 / Foundations

The Scope of Psychology

Psychology is the scientific study of behavior and mental processes. Before trusting any claim made "in the name of psychology," you need to know two things: what's already firmly established, and what counts as real scientific evidence.

▸Brain damage & face recognition

Damage to a specific region on the right side of the brain can wipe out face recognition while leaving everything else intact — a condition called prosopagnosia.

Real caseOliver Sacks described a man with prosopagnosia who complained to a waiter that someone was staring at him — it was his own reflection in a mirror.

Takeaway: psychological functions like face recognition are localized to specific brain regions.

▸Attributing traits — the Fundamental Attribution Error

A woman donates to charity in a crowded store while everyone watches. Most observers call her "generous" — even though the social pressure was strong enough that almost anyone would have donated.

BiasWe overestimate personality and underestimate the situation when explaining other people's behavior — the Fundamental Attribution Error (a.k.a. dispositional bias).

The twist: for our own behavior we flip — overestimating situational causes (good performance = "I'm skilled," bad performance = "the test was unfair").

▸Obesity & deprivation

Rats deprived of food, then returned to normal weight, then given free access — eat more than rats with no deprivation history. A history of restriction seems to recalibrate eating behavior.

▸Media violence — testing the "cathartic effect" myth

Some believe watching violence lets kids "get it out of their system" (the cathartic effect). The evidence disagrees:

  • Kids who watched violent cartoons became more aggressive with peers.
  • Kids who watched nonviolent cartoons showed no change.
  • Long-term: more violent TV at age 9 → more aggression at age 19 (Eron et al., 1972).
02 / Roots

Historical Origins of Psychology

Two questions drove the field's beginnings: is knowledge inborn or learned? (nature–nurture), and can the mind be studied scientifically at all?

▸The Nature–Nurture Debate

Nature view

Knowledge is inborn, accessed through reasoning. Descartes argued ideas like God, the self, geometry, and infinity are innate. He also framed the body as a machine — the seed of today's information-processing models.

Nurture view

The mind at birth is a tabula rasa ("blank slate"). Locke argued experience "writes" knowledge onto it — the root of associationist psychology (and modern memory/learning research).

▸Wundt & Introspection

Wilhelm Wundt founded the first experimental psychology lab (Leipzig, 1879). He used introspection — observing and reporting your own perceptions, thoughts, and feelings — but paired it with controlled experiments.

Limitation: trained observers still gave wildly different introspective reports about the same simple stimulus, so the method proved unworkable for rapid mental events. This dissatisfaction helped spark the schools below.

A chronological walk through the early schools

17th century

Descartes (nature) vs. Locke (nurture)

Philosophical roots of the nature–nurture debate.

1879

Wundt's lab opens in Leipzig

Introspection + experiment becomes the first scientific method in psychology.

Late 1800s–1910s

Structuralism vs. Functionalism

Titchener's structuralism breaks consciousness into elements. James's functionalism asks what consciousness is for — adaptation — and pushes psychology toward studying actual behavior. Both still treat psychology as the science of conscious experience.

~1912–1920

Behaviorism, Gestalt & Psychoanalysis displace them

Watson's behaviorism rejects private consciousness as unscientific — only public, observable behavior counts. In Germany, Wertheimer, Koffka & Köhler found that perception depends on the whole pattern, not isolated parts (Gestalt). Meanwhile Freud's psychoanalysis argues hidden, unconscious sexual/aggressive impulses drive behavior.

20th century onward

Information processing, psycholinguistics, neuropsychology

These later movements feed directly into today's five contemporary perspectives →

▸Behaviorism

Watson: behavior is public and measurable; consciousness is private and unscientific. Nearly all behavior = conditioning shaped by reinforcement.

ExampleGiving a child cookies to stop whining rewards whining — reinforcing the very habit you wanted to stop.

Smallest unit: the conditioned response. Gave rise to Stimulus–Response (S–R) terminology.

▸Gestalt Psychology

Gestalt = "form/configuration." Core principle: the whole is different from the sum of its parts, because the relationships between parts matter.

ExampleThree angles of an equilateral triangle aren't seen as "three angles" — they're seen as one triangle.

Later shaped social psychology through Lewin, Asch, and Heider, who applied "wholes over parts" to how we perceive people.

▸Psychoanalysis

Freud proposed that childhood's forbidden wishes get pushed into the unconscious, where they keep shaping thoughts, feelings, and actions — leaking out through dreams, slips of the tongue, and mannerisms.

Therapy tools: free association (say whatever comes to mind) and dream analysis — both designed to surface unconscious material.

03 / Today's lenses

Five Contemporary Psychological Perspectives

A perspective is a lens, not a verdict — perspectives aren't mutually exclusive, and most real topics need an eclectic approach that blends several. Click a lens below to see how each one re-explains the same event: punching someone after they insult you.

SCENARIO → Following an insult, you punch someone in the face.

The Biological Perspective

Describe the act in terms of brain areas firing and the nerve signals that activate your arm's muscles. Focus: neurobiological processes underlying behavior — e.g. explaining depression via neurotransmitter imbalance, or childhood amnesia via an immature hippocampus.

▸Biological

Explains behavior via neurobiological processes — neurotransmitters, brain structures.

ExampleDepression linked to abnormal neurotransmitter levels; the hippocampus (still immature in infants) explains childhood amnesia.

▸Behavioral

We learn to do things because of what happens after we do them — conditioning and reinforcement.

ExampleA teacher gives stickers for correct answers → you try harder to answer correctly. That's behaviorism in action.

▸Cognitive

Focuses on mental processes — perceiving, remembering, reasoning, deciding, problem solving — often using a mind-as-computer analogy.

ExampleIncoming information gets selected, compared, combined with memory, and transformed.

▸Psychoanalytic

Behavior stems from unconscious motives — beliefs, fears, desires we aren't aware of, often rooted in childhood.

Example"Forgetting" a dentist appointment may really be an unconscious wish to avoid it.

▸Subjectivist

Behavior is a function of the perceived world, not the objective one. To understand someone, you need their personal "definition of the situation" — shaped by culture, history, and motivation. Most open to individual and cultural differences of all five perspectives.

ExampleThree people witness the same car accident: one feels tragedy, one concludes "people drive too fast here," one — a past survivor of a similar crash — has a panic attack. Same event, three different psychological realities.
04 / Quick recall

Key Terminology

Scan these the night before the exam — if you can define each in one breath, you're ready.

Psychology
The scientific study of behavior and mental processes.
Introspection
Observing and reporting your own perceptions, thoughts, and feelings.
Tabula Rasa
"Blank slate" — Locke's idea that the mind starts empty and experience fills it.
Prosopagnosia
Inability to recognize familiar faces after brain damage, despite normal functioning otherwise.
Fundamental Attribution Error
Overestimating personality, underestimating situation, when judging others' behavior.
Overjustification Effect
An external reward undermines behavior that was previously driven by intrinsic motivation.
Cathartic Effect
The (largely unsupported) idea that watching violence reduces aggression by "releasing" it vicariously.
Stimulus–Response (S–R)
Terminology, not a theory — describing behavior in terms of stimuli and responses.
Unconscious
Thoughts, impulses, and emotions we're unaware of but that still influence us (Freud).
Gestalt
German for "form" — the principle that perception organizes parts into meaningful wholes.
Free Association
Therapy technique: say whatever comes to mind, to surface unconscious material.
Eclectic Approach
Combining multiple perspectives, since they explain different facets of the same behavior.

Practice Questions

Five scenario-based questions in the same case-study format as your reference exam paper — each with multi-part (a/b/c) analytical sub-questions and a tap-to-reveal answer key.

1

Cousins, Different Worlds

Anika and Tahmid are first cousins. Anika displayed an unusual intuitive grasp of geometry and numbers from age four, before any formal schooling — she would arrange blocks into symmetrical patterns no one had taught her. Tahmid, raised since infancy by his immigrant grandparents, speaks with their exact idioms, holds their food habits and superstitions, and mirrors their cautious, deferential manner with strangers — traits absent in the rest of his immediate family.

a

Explain how the nature–nurture debate applies to Anika's and Tahmid's traits, referencing Descartes and Locke. 05

Show answer
  1. Anika → Nature view: her geometric intuition appears before any teaching, echoing Descartes' claim that some knowledge (he specifically names geometric axioms) is innate, accessed through reasoning rather than learned.
  2. Tahmid → Nurture view: his idioms, food habits, and manner are not innate — they were "written onto" him through years of exposure to his grandparents, matching Locke's tabula rasa: the mind starts blank and experience fills it.
  3. Conclusion: the same family can illustrate both sides of the debate — neither cousin proves nature or nurture "wins"; each shows one mechanism operating strongly in that individual's development.
b

Explain the limitations a researcher would face trying to study Anika's ability using Wundt's method of introspection. 07

Show answer
  • Introspection asks a person to observe and report their own perceptions/thoughts — but Anika's process is likely rapid and automatic, the exact category Wundt's method struggled with.
  • Even trained adults give inconsistent introspective reports about identical simple stimuli — a young child like Anika would be even less reliable, making her self-report scientifically unworkable.
  • This is why introspection alone is no longer central to the modern cognitive perspective, which instead infers mental processes from observable behavior and performance.
c

Identify which historical school of psychology (structuralism, functionalism, behaviorism, Gestalt, or psychoanalysis) best frames Tahmid's learned behavior, and justify. 06

Show answer

Behaviorism best frames Tahmid's case.

  • His idioms, habits, and manner were acquired through repeated exposure and reinforcement within his grandparents' household — consistent with Watson's claim that nearly all behavior results from conditioning shaped by the environment.
  • Each habit (a phrase, a custom) functions like a small conditioned response, built up into the larger, complex pattern of behavior we now see as "Tahmid's personality."
  • (Functionalism could be argued as a secondary point — these habits also serve an adaptive purpose, helping Tahmid fit into his grandparents' world — but behaviorism is the stronger primary fit since the focus is on how the behavior was acquired.)
2

The Study Group Walkout

Farhan, normally an active member of his university study group, abruptly stops attending after a heated argument with a groupmate. He avoids messages from the group, claims he's "just busy," but friends notice he tenses up and changes the subject whenever the argument is mentioned.

a

Explain Farhan's withdrawal using all five contemporary psychological perspectives. 12

Show answer
  • Biological: the argument may have triggered a stress response (e.g. cortisol/nervous system activation) that Farhan now associates with the group, producing physical tension when it's mentioned.
  • Behavioral: avoiding the group removes the discomfort of the argument — this relief reinforces the avoidance, making Farhan more likely to keep avoiding the group.
  • Cognitive: Farhan may be replaying the argument, reasoning about how others perceived him, and deciding that avoidance is the safest plan — his behavior follows from this internal mental processing, not just the event itself.
  • Psychoanalytic: his claim of "just busy" may be a surface excuse masking an unconscious wish to avoid the humiliation or anger stirred up by the conflict — visible instead through his physical tension (a "leak" of unconscious feeling).
  • Subjectivist: what matters is not the argument itself but Farhan's personal interpretation of it — if he experienced it as a deep personal insult, his withdrawal makes sense even if an outside observer thinks the disagreement was minor.
b

Identify which perspective is described as "most open to individual and cultural differences," and justify using Farhan's case. 05

Show answer

The subjectivist perspective. It explicitly centers each person's own "definition of the situation," which varies by culture, personal history, and current motivation — so two people in Farhan's exact situation could react completely differently depending on what the argument means to them personally.

c

Explain the concept of an "eclectic approach" and why fully understanding Farhan's behavior might require it. 06

Show answer

An eclectic approach combines multiple perspectives because they aren't mutually exclusive — each highlights a different layer of the same complex event. No single lens captures everything: biology explains the physical stress response, behaviorism explains why avoidance persists, cognition explains the reasoning behind his choice, psychoanalysis explains the hidden emotional driver, and subjectivism explains why this particular event hit him so hard. Together, not separately, they give the full picture.

3

The Charity Stall

At an office charity drive, Maliha donates a generous amount in full view of her colleagues, many of whom are also donating under visible social pressure. Separately, Rafiq has always voluntarily stayed late to mentor new juniors — but after his manager begins awarding "bonus points" for mentoring, Rafiq starts doing it noticeably less often, and only when points are explicitly offered.

a

Explain, using the Fundamental Attribution Error, how Maliha's colleagues are likely to interpret her donation — and why that judgment may be inaccurate. 05

Show answer

Colleagues will likely conclude Maliha is simply "a generous person" — attributing her donation to personality/disposition. This may be inaccurate because the situation itself (everyone watching, everyone else donating) created strong social pressure that would push almost anyone to donate, regardless of how generous they actually are. This is the Fundamental Attribution Error: overestimating personality, underestimating the situational pressure, when explaining someone else's behavior.

b

Explain how Maliha would likely explain her own donation differently from how her colleagues explain it. 04

Show answer

When explaining our own behavior, we tend to flip the bias — overestimating situational causes. Maliha is more likely to say "everyone was watching, I felt I had to," rather than "I'm just a generous person" — even if she would happily call someone else generous for doing the same thing.

c

Explain Rafiq's change in behavior using the Overjustification Effect. 04

Show answer

Rafiq originally mentored juniors out of intrinsic motivation (he simply wanted to help). Once an external reward (bonus points) was attached, his sense of "why" shifted — the reward became the perceived reason for the behavior. When the internal motivation gets crowded out by the external one, the behavior actually decreases outside of reward conditions — exactly what happens when Rafiq now only mentors "when points are explicitly offered." This is the Overjustification Effect.

4

Testing the Cathartic Effect

A school counselor is debating two competing theories about violent video games among teenagers: one camp believes the games increase real-world aggression; another believes playing lets students "release" aggression harmlessly (the cathartic effect), similar to old debates about televised violence.

a

Design a basic experiment (modeled on the Eron et al. media-violence study) the counselor could run, identifying the independent variable, dependent variable, and comparison groups. 06

Show answer
  • Independent variable (IV): type of game content played (violent vs. nonviolent), held for an equal amount of time across groups.
  • Dependent variable (DV): observed aggression in peer interactions afterward (e.g. rated by teachers/researchers).
  • Group 1: teenagers play a violent video game for a set period.
  • Group 2: teenagers play a nonviolent video game for the same period.
  • Behavior is then compared immediately after, mirroring the original cartoon-violence study design.
b

Explain, based on established research findings, the predicted result — and why the cathartic effect theory is not well supported. 05

Show answer

Based on the original cartoon-violence research, we'd predict the violent-game group becomes more aggressive afterward, while the nonviolent-game group shows no change — the opposite of what the cathartic effect predicts. Research has consistently failed to support catharsis: watching/playing violent content does not "drain" aggression — it tends to model and reinforce it instead.

c

Explain why a long-term, multi-year study would add value beyond this single short-term experiment. 05

Show answer

A short-term experiment only shows an immediate effect. The classic study tracking boys from age 9 to 19 showed that early violent-TV preference predicted aggression a decade later — revealing a lasting, cumulative relationship rather than a momentary spike. Long-term data is essential for arguing the effect isn't just temporary excitement, but something that shapes behavior over development.

5

Two Researchers, One Patient

Following a head injury, a patient can no longer recognize even his own children's faces, though he reads, talks, and recalls old memories perfectly normally. Two researchers later study his recovery process differently: Dr. Hasan carefully catalogues the patient's reported sensory impressions piece by piece, while Dr. Nabila is more interested in why the patient's new coping habits emerged and what purpose they serve in his daily life.

a

Identify which historical school of psychology Dr. Hasan's approach aligns with, versus Dr. Nabila's, and justify. 06

Show answer
  • Dr. Hasan → Structuralism: breaking experience down into its basic elements through careful, descriptive self-report mirrors Titchener's structuralist method.
  • Dr. Nabila → Functionalism: asking why a behavior emerged and what adaptive purpose it serves matches William James's functionalist focus on how the mind helps a person adapt to their environment.
b

Explain, using Gestalt psychology, how the patient's face perception may be disrupted while his perception of other objects stays normal. 07

Show answer

Gestalt psychology holds that "the whole is different from the sum of its parts" — perception depends on the relationships among features, not the features in isolation. Recognizing a face requires binding individual features (eyes, nose, mouth, spacing) into one coherent whole — exactly like seeing three angles as a single triangle rather than three separate lines. If the brain region responsible for assembling that specific "whole" (face-Gestalt) is damaged, the patient can still process individual visual elements — explaining why other objects, made of differently organized wholes, remain perfectly recognizable.

c

Referencing prosopagnosia from the chapter, explain what this case reveals about brain function. 05

Show answer

This mirrors the chapter's classic case of prosopagnosia — face recognition lost while other abilities (reading, speech, memory) remain intact. It demonstrates localization of function: specific psychological abilities are tied to specific, distinct brain regions, rather than the brain working as one undifferentiated whole. Damage to one localized region can knock out one specific capability while leaving the rest of cognition fully functional.
