Foundations Nature vs. Nurture

The oldest debate in development: is who we become written in our genes, or built by our experiences? Modern psychology says both, interacting continuously — this is the lens you apply to almost every exam scenario involving "why is this child different."

Historical Positions

  • John Locke — Tabula Rasa: the mind starts as a "blank slate"; all knowledge comes from experience (pure nurture).
  • Charles Darwin: evolution highlighted the biological basis of development (pro-nature).
  • Behaviorism (Watson & Skinner): claimed environment is everything — Watson said he could train any healthy infant into any profession regardless of ancestry.
Exam Tip The modern, "correct" answer to any nature-nurture question is interactionist: genes set a range of possibilities, and environment determines where within that range a person lands — and at what speed they get there.

How the Interaction Plays Out

Brain Development

100 billion neurons at birth, brain triples in weight by age 3 — wiring depends on genetics and environmental stimulation/deprivation.

Maturation

Genetically-timed sequences (fetal development, puberty) that unfold mostly independent of environment — but illness, malnutrition, or substances can disrupt them.

Motor Development

Kipsigis (Kenya): active teaching → walk 3–5 weeks early. Ache (Paraguay): little early practice → walk ~1 year late, but catch up later.

Speech Development

European infants talk ~1 yr (rewarded). Children in San Marcos (Guatemala) get little verbal interaction and don't speak until 2+.

Key Insight In every cross-cultural example above, environment changes the rate of skill acquisition — not the final ceiling of ability. This single sentence answers most "explain the role of environment" questions.

Foundations Stages, Critical & Sensitive Periods

What "Stage" Means

  1. Behaviors cluster around one dominant theme.
  2. Each stage is qualitatively different from the ones before/after it.
  3. Everyone passes through stages in the same order (environment changes speed, not sequence).

Critical vs. Sensitive Periods

TypeDefinitionExample
Critical PeriodA window in which a specific event must happen, or normal development fails entirely.6–7 weeks post-conception: critical for sex organ formation.
Sensitive PeriodAn optimal window; missing it limits — but doesn't always prevent — full development.First year: optimal for attachment. Before age 6–7: optimal for language acquisition.

Infancy Capacities of the Newborn

Contrary to William James's "blooming, buzzing confusion," newborns arrive with functioning senses and built-in learning ability.

Vision

  • Poor acuity at birth (very nearsighted); reaches adult-level by 7–8 months.
  • Scans high-contrast edges; prefers curved/complex patterns.
  • Prefers normal over scrambled faces, and happy over fearful faces — but doesn't yet prefer normal over "rabbit-like" distorted faces (that preference appears by 3 months).

Hearing

  • Fetuses (26–28 weeks) react to loud sounds; newborns orient toward sound.
  • Prefer mother's voice and prenatal stories — they've learned rhythm, not content.
  • Motherese (baby talk): high pitch, exaggerated contours, longer pauses — helps infants find word boundaries.

Taste & Smell

Example: A baby relaxes and smiles at sugar water but scrunches its face and sticks out its tongue at a bitter taste — an innate disgust reflex protecting against toxins.
  • Prefers sweet; rejects sour/bitter via distinct facial reflexes.
  • Turns toward mother's own milk scent after days of nursing; even bottle-fed babies prefer breast-milk odor over formula (innate, survival-linked preference).

Learning & Memory

  • Hours-old infants learn simple cause-effect rules and adapt quickly when rules reverse.
  • 3-month-olds remember which limb moves a mobile — 8 days later.
  • Babies prefer female voices generally, but show no special preference for father's voice (only mother's).

Childhood Cognitive Development

Piaget saw children as "inquiring scientists" building schemas — mental theories about how the world works — through assimilation (fitting new info into existing schemas) and accommodation (changing the schema itself).

Example: A child thinks "firefighter = man" (schema). On meeting a female firefighter, the child updates the schema to include women — that's accommodation.

Piaget's Four Stages

0–2 yrs
Sensorimotor

Learns through actions and their consequences. Develops object permanence gradually: nonexistent if hidden (<8mo) → searches in old spot (10mo) → searches correctly in last-seen spot (~1yr).

2–7 yrs
Preoperational

Symbolic thinking emerges (a stick "is" a horse). Limited by irreversibility, lack of conservation (spread-out checkers seem "more"), and egocentrism (fails the three-mountain task).

7–11 yrs
Concrete Operational

Masters conservation, can order/seriate objects and build mental maps — but abstract reasoning still needs concrete objects to anchor it.

12+ yrs
Formal Operational

Abstract, hypothetical, and systematic reasoning (e.g., testing all variables in the pendulum experiment).

Critiques of Piaget

  • Underestimated infants: 4.5-month-olds show surprise at "impossible" events — hinting at earlier object permanence.
  • Task demands: wording/phrasing (e.g. "army" vs "soldiers") affects performance independent of true ability.
  • Culture matters: schooling and culture shift mastery timing — development is more continuous and context-dependent than rigid stages suggest.

Alternative Theories

TheoryCore Idea
Information-ProcessingCognitive growth = mastering specific skills (memory, attention); "neo-Piagetians" allow domain-specific stages.
Knowledge-AcquisitionKids & adults think similarly; differences come from knowledge, not capacity. Overlapping Waves (Siegler): multiple strategies used at once, weaker ones phased out.
Sociocultural (Vygotsky)Zone of Proximal Development: gap between solo performance and guided performance. Private speech (self-talk) and culture shape learning.

Theory of Mind (ToM)

Understanding that other people hold their own (sometimes false) beliefs.

AgeMilestone
Age 2Grasps that others have desires and perceptions.
Age 3Recognizes false beliefs exist, but still focuses on desires — fails the "Candy Box" test (assumes everyone knows crayons are inside).
Age 4+Understands beliefs (even false ones) drive behavior — passes the Candy Box test.

Clinical link: Autistic individuals often show persistent difficulty inferring others' thoughts/intentions — a core ToM deficit.

Kohlberg's Moral Reasoning Stages

LevelStageOrientation
I. Preconventional (<10)1Punishment — obey to avoid trouble.
2Reward — conform to get favors/benefits.
II. Conventional (10–13)3Good-boy/good-girl — seek social approval.
4Authority — uphold laws to avoid guilt/censure.
III. Postconventional (adolescence+)5Social contract — laws serve justice, can be changed if unfair.
6Universal ethics — human dignity outranks the law.
Memorization Trick Kohlberg = "Me → Us → Principle." Preconventional is about consequences to ME, conventional is about fitting into US (society), postconventional is about abstract PRINCIPLES above any specific society.

Childhood Personality & Social Development

Temperament

Babies are born with stable personality tendencies that, in turn, shape how parents respond to them — not the other way around as once believed.

Easy
~40%

Happy, regular sleep/eating, adapts easily to new things.

Difficult
~10%

Fussy, irregular routines, resists change.

Slow-to-Warm-Up
~15%

Shy and cautious; needs time to adjust to novelty.

Mixed
~35%

Doesn't fit clearly into one category.

  • Stability: infant temperament (0–2) doesn't strongly predict later personality, but from toddlerhood onward it starts to (e.g., shy toddlers often become less-aggressive teens).
  • Nature & Nurture: identical twins resemble each other more than fraternal twins (genes), and twins raised together are more alike than those raised apart (environment) — both forces operate.
  • Goodness of Fit: healthy development depends on how well a child's temperament matches the parent's style — a patient parent can help even a "difficult" baby thrive.

Childhood Attachment Theory

Harlow's Monkey Experiment overturned the old "love = food" theory: baby monkeys overwhelmingly chose a soft cloth surrogate (comfort) over a wire mother that dispensed milk. Comfort and closeness are core attachment drivers, not just feeding.

Ainsworth's "Strange Situation" — Attachment Styles

Style% of InfantsBehaviorLikely Cause
Secure~60%Happy to see mom return, seeks comfort briefly, then resumes play.Responsive, predictable caregiving.
Avoidant~15%Ignores mom; acts indifferent.Insecure attachment, often dismissive/unresponsive caregiving.
Ambivalent~10%Clings then resists — cries to be held, then pushes away.Inconsistent caregiving.
Disorganized~15%Confused, scared, may freeze.Often linked to abuse or neglect (rare, most severe).
Long-Term Effects Attachment style tends to stay stable unless family circumstances change drastically. Securely attached children handle stress better and stay curious; insecurely attached children may give up easily and struggle to trust others later in life — directly relevant to case-study questions about adult relationship/trust issues.

Gender Identity & Gender Typing

Gender Identity = internal sense of one's own gender. Gender Typing = learning the behaviors/roles society links to a gender.

TheoryCore MechanismKey Limitation
Social LearningRewards, punishments, and imitation of the same-sex parent.Doesn't explain why kids police gender rules more harshly than adults.
Cognitive-DevelopmentalKids actively act on identity once they grasp Gender Constancy (age 6–7) — gender stays fixed despite appearance changes.Kids show gendered behavior well before they understand constancy.

Influences: parents (different praise/dress/punishment by sex — e.g., dads discouraging "girly" behavior in sons) and peers (who police each other's gender behavior even more harshly).

Question 1

Cognitive Development
A daycare teacher tests two children with a classic experiment: she pours water from a short, wide glass into a tall, thin glass in front of both. Mira (age 4) insists the tall glass now has "more water." Tariq (age 9) immediately says, "It's still the same amount — you just poured it." Separately, when the teacher hides a toy under a blanket, her 7-month-old sibling, baby Zayn, stops looking for it entirely, as if it vanished.
a) Identify the Piagetian stage each child (Mira, Tariq) and the infant (Zayn) is in, and the specific cognitive limitation/ability each one is demonstrating. (08 marks)
b) Explain how a critic of Piaget (using Information-Processing, Knowledge-Acquisition, or Sociocultural approaches) might interpret Mira's response differently than Piaget did. (07 marks)
c) Using Vygotsky's concept of the Zone of Proximal Development, explain how the teacher could help Mira move toward understanding conservation. (06 marks)
Show Model Answer
Part a) Stage identification
  • Zayn (7 months): Sensorimotor stage. He is showing a lack of fully developed object permanence — before about 8 months, infants treat hidden objects as if they no longer exist.
  • Mira (4 years): Preoperational stage. She demonstrates lack of conservation — she focuses on the visual appearance (taller glass = more) rather than logical reasoning, and shows irreversibility (can't mentally reverse the pouring action).
  • Tariq (9 years): Concrete Operational stage. He has mastered conservation — he understands that quantity is conserved despite a change in container shape, and can mentally reverse the transformation.
Part b) Critique application
  • A Knowledge-Acquisition theorist would argue Mira's failure isn't about a fixed cognitive "stage ceiling" but about task wording or knowledge gaps — children often perform conservation tasks better when language is simplified or made more concrete.
  • Alternatively, an Information-Processing theorist would say Mira simply hasn't yet mastered the specific skill of attention-switching needed to track both height and width simultaneously — it's a skill deficit, not a stage-bound limitation.
  • Either way, the critique challenges Piaget's claim that all children pass through rigid, universal stages at fixed ages.
Part c) Zone of Proximal Development
  • The ZPD is the gap between what Mira can do unaided and what she can do with guided support.
  • The teacher could scaffold Mira's understanding by physically pouring the water back into the original glass with Mira watching, asking guiding questions ("Did we add or take away any water?"), and using private-speech prompts Mira can repeat to herself.
  • This guided interaction can help Mira move closer to independently grasping conservation, even before she would have arrived there on her own.

Question 2

Moral Development
Two students are caught in separate incidents at school. Bina (age 6) accidentally knocked over and broke an entire shelf of 12 glasses while trying to help set the table; she insists she should be punished "a lot" because so many glasses broke. Meanwhile, Faruk (age 16) was caught downloading a banned book online; when questioned, he argues, "The law banning this book is unjust and violates basic human rights to knowledge — I'd break it again."
a) Using Piaget's stages of moral judgment, explain Bina's reasoning about her punishment. (06 marks)
b) Using Kohlberg's stages of moral reasoning, identify and explain which stage Faruk's reasoning reflects. (08 marks)
c) Compare how Piaget and Kohlberg's frameworks would each explain the progression from childhood to Faruk's adolescent reasoning. (06 marks)
Show Model Answer
Part a) Piaget — Bina
  • Bina is in the Moral Absolutism stage (~5–7 years).
  • This stage judges wrongdoing by the severity of consequences, not the intent behind the action — exactly like Piaget's classic example of breaking 10 cups by accident being judged "worse" than deliberately stealing jam.
  • Bina focuses on the number of broken glasses (the outcome) rather than recognizing her good intention (helping set the table), which is the hallmark of this stage.
Part b) Kohlberg — Faruk
  • Faruk's reasoning reflects Level III: Postconventional, Stage 6 (Ethical Principle Orientation).
  • He appeals to a universal ethical principle (the right to knowledge/human dignity) that he believes supersedes the actual written law — this is the defining feature of Stage 6.
  • This differs from Stage 5 (social-contract), which would argue laws should be changed through legitimate process; Faruk goes further by asserting his moral principle justifies breaking the law even now.
Part c) Comparing the frameworks
  • Piaget's framework explains the shift as cognitive: children move from rigid rule-worship to flexible, consensus-based, and eventually principled thinking as their cognitive capacity for abstract reasoning matures (paralleling formal operational thought).
  • Kohlberg's framework is more specifically about moral reasoning maturity, showing a progression from self-interest (punishment/reward) to social conformity (approval/authority) to self-chosen ethical principles — Faruk's case shows reasoning has moved beyond simply "the law says so" toward independent ethical judgment.
  • Both frameworks agree that moral reasoning becomes less rigid and more abstract/principled with cognitive maturity.

Question 3

Gender Identity & Self-Concept
Parents notice their 4-year-old son, Adib, insists on only playing with trucks and refuses anything "pink," even though he doesn't yet understand that he will always be a boy regardless of his haircut or clothing. Meanwhile, his older sister Lina (age 10) has started comparing her grades and sports ability constantly to her classmates, saying things like, "I'm okay at math but everyone is better at running than me."
a) Using the theories of gender typing, explain Adib's gendered behavior and identify the theoretical limitation it illustrates. (07 marks)
b) Identify the stage of self-concept and self-esteem development that Lina is demonstrating, and explain why this shift occurs at her age. (06 marks)
c) Explain how parents and peers might be reinforcing Adib's gender-typed behavior. (05 marks)
Show Model Answer
Part a) Gender typing theories
  • Adib's strong preference for trucks and rejection of "pink" reflects Gender Schema Theory — he has built a mental "gender filter" that automatically sorts toys/colors into "boy" and "girl" categories and organizes his identity around that filter.
  • This is also a key limitation of Cognitive-Developmental Theory: that theory predicts gendered behavior should only emerge once a child grasps Gender Constancy (age 6–7), but Adib shows strong gendered preferences well before that age — proving gendered behavior can appear before constancy is understood.
Part b) Lina's self-concept/self-esteem stage
  • Lina is in Middle Childhood (9–12 years) for self-esteem: she compares herself directly to peers ("better at running than me") rather than simply rating her own skill in isolation.
  • For self-concept, she fits the 8+ years stage of describing herself with complex, comparative traits rather than the simple "I am good at X" of younger children.
  • This shift happens because cognitive development now allows social comparison and a more realistic, less inflated self-evaluation than the overconfidence typical of preschoolers.
Part c) Parent/peer reinforcement
  • Parents may reinforce this through Social Learning mechanisms: rewarding/praising "boy" behavior (e.g., trucks), discouraging "girly" interests, and modeling same-sex parent behavior for Adib to imitate.
  • Peers likely reinforce it even more harshly than parents by teasing or excluding Adib if he plays with "girl" toys, which is a well-documented pattern of children policing gender norms more rigidly than adults do.

Question 4

Temperament & Attachment
Twin sisters Hana and Noor were both described by their mother as "fussy babies who hated any change in routine" from birth. As toddlers, in Ainsworth's Strange Situation test, Hana cried when her mother left but calmed down quickly and resumed play upon her return. Noor, however, clung tightly to her mother on return, crying to be picked up, then squirmed angrily to be put down again moments later. Their mother responded patiently and consistently to Hana, but was often inconsistent and unpredictable in responding to Noor's needs.
a) Identify the twins' shared temperament type at birth, according to Thomas & Chess. (04 marks)
b) Using Ainsworth's attachment styles, classify Hana's and Noor's attachment patterns and explain the caregiving differences that likely produced each outcome. (08 marks)
c) Using the Goodness of Fit theory, explain why Hana developed secure attachment despite a "difficult" temperament, while Noor did not. (06 marks)
Show Model Answer
Part a) Temperament type
  • Both twins show the Difficult temperament type (~10% of infants in Thomas & Chess's study): fussy, irregular, and resistant to change.
Part b) Attachment classification
  • Hana — Secure attachment (~60%): she shows distress on separation but is easily comforted and resumes play on reunion — the classic secure pattern, produced by her mother's responsive, predictable caregiving.
  • Noor — Ambivalent attachment (~10%): she clings then resists comfort (crying to be held, then pushing away) — this push-pull pattern is produced by inconsistent caregiving, where the child can't predict whether comfort will be reliably available.
Part c) Goodness of Fit
  • Goodness of Fit theory states that healthy development depends on the match between a child's temperament and the parenting style they receive, not the temperament alone.
  • Although both twins started with a "difficult" temperament, Hana's mother provided patient, consistent responses that created a good fit, allowing Hana to become securely attached despite her fussy nature.
  • Noor received inconsistent responses — a poor fit for a temperament that already struggles with unpredictability — which likely drove her toward an ambivalent, insecure attachment style. This shows temperament alone doesn't determine outcome; the interaction with caregiving style does.

Question 5

Adolescent Identity
Three 18-year-old cousins are asked about their future plans. Samira says, "I'm definitely going to be an engineer like my father — I've never really thought about anything else, it's just expected." Yusuf says, "I have no idea what I want to do, and honestly I don't think about it much — whatever happens, happens." Their cousin Dina says, "I spent the last two years trying journalism, then volunteering in healthcare, and now I'm seriously considering psychology — I still haven't fully decided, but I'm actively exploring."
a) Using Marcia's Identity Statuses, classify each cousin (Samira, Yusuf, Dina) and justify your classification using the presence/absence of crisis and commitment. (09 marks)
b) Explain what would need to change in Samira's experience for her to move toward Identity Achievement. (04 marks)
c) Explain one potential psychological risk associated with Yusuf's current identity status if it persists into adulthood. (05 marks)
Show Model Answer
Part a) Identity status classification
  • Samira — Foreclosure: she has a clear commitment (engineering) but has never undergone a personal crisis or exploration; she has simply adopted her father's path without questioning it.
  • Yusuf — Identity Diffusion: he shows no crisis (no active exploration) and no commitment — he is indifferent to the question of his future direction.
  • Dina — Moratorium: she is actively in crisis, exploring multiple paths (journalism, healthcare, psychology), but has not yet committed to one — the defining feature of moratorium.
Part b) Path to Identity Achievement for Samira
  • Samira would need to actively question and explore her assumed path — for example, by considering alternative careers, examining whether engineering truly reflects her own values and interests rather than her father's — before either recommitting to engineering with full ownership or choosing a different path. Only after this crisis-and-resolution process would she reach Identity Achievement.
Part c) Risk of persistent Identity Diffusion
  • If Yusuf's diffusion persists, he risks ongoing difficulty forming a stable sense of self, which can manifest as low motivation, poor long-term planning, difficulty committing to relationships or careers, and increased vulnerability to anxiety or low self-worth in adulthood, since he lacks the internal compass that a resolved identity typically provides.
