Part 1 — Study Notes

1.1 What Is Communication?

Communication is the process of sending and receiving messages. It only counts as effective once the message is actually understood — and ideally once it stimulates action or pushes the receiver toward new thinking. Sending a message isn't the same as communicating; the loop has to close.

Real-life application: A manager who emails instructions but never checks whether the team understood them has only sent a message — not necessarily communicated.

1.2 Why Communication Skills Matter

To you

  • Your workplace performance is judged largely by how well you communicate, not just your technical output.
  • There's a strong correlation between communication skill and income.
  • Technology magnifies exposure — a poorly written email or message is now seen by far more people than before, so the stakes of every message are higher.

To business

Even a one-person business is an economic and social system. Coordinating employees, suppliers, customers, legal advisors, communities, and government agencies all happens through communication.

Likely external audiences a business must reach

Audience GroupIncludes
Core Business PartnersSuppliers, contract workers, manufacturers, shippers, distributors
CustomersConsumers, business customers, the government
Public GroupsCommunity groups, citizen groups, NGOs, schools, foundations
Regulatory AgentsGovernment, trade alliances, unions, national/international legal experts
Industry PartnersCompetitors, similar businesses, lobbyists

Three current challenges facing business communicators

  1. New information technologies — requires verbal, information, technological, and visual literacy to keep up.
  2. Increasingly global business — different attitudes toward punctuality, individualism vs. collectivism, religion, and hierarchy mean cross-cultural communicators must learn and honor others' orientations rather than assume their own is universal.
  3. Growing diversity & rising ethical expectations — the internet creates fast, wide transparency for company behavior, pushing the rise of Corporate Social Responsibility (CSR) functions.

1.3 Three Categories of Business Communication

CategoryWhat it coversExample
Internal-operationalWork-related communication within the business — goals, instructions, oral exchanges among staffA supervisor briefing the team on a new process
External-operationalWork-related communication with parties outside the businessNegotiating terms with a supplier
PersonalNon-operational communication that builds and sustains relationships and shapes employee attitudesCongratulating a colleague, casual check-ins
Personal communication isn't "extra" — it directly affects employee attitudes, and attitudes directly affect productivity.

1.4 Communication Networks: Formal vs. Informal

NetworkNatureCarries
Formal NetworkEstablished, predictable channels — like the body's main blood vesselsReports, memos, official email, orders, instructions, newsletters, intranet
Informal Network ("the grapevine")Unofficial, follows no set pattern, constantly shifting groups and overlapping membershipsPersonal exchanges that may or may not support the formal message — can be accurate or distorted

Grapevine = informal communication. It often moves faster than the formal network, especially when official information is slow or withheld.

1.5 Channels & Media of Communication

Letters · Memos · Reports · Notice boards · Faxes · Telephone · Email · Face-to-face · Body language · Video/video-conferencing · Internet

Channel richness matters: face-to-face and video carry tone and body language (high richness); memos and email carry only words (low richness). Since 55% of meaning is read non-verbally, low-richness channels are far more prone to misinterpretation for sensitive or urgent messages.

Factors affecting how much a business communicates

  • Nature of the business (courier service vs. insurance company vs. cleaning service)
  • Business size and complexity
  • Operating plan / business location
  • People — individual, unique communication needs

1.6 The Business Communication Process

Every act of business communication moves through ten steps, split across two communicators, and is shaped by a larger context (business-economic, sociocultural, historical) plus each person's own world (organizational, professional, personal).

Communicator 1 (sender)Communicator 2 (receiver)
1. Senses a communication need7. Receives the message
2. Defines the problem/situation8. Interprets the message
3. Searches for possible solutions9. Decides on a response
4. Selects a course of action (message type, content, style, format, channel)10. May send a responding message
5. Composes the message
6. Sends/delivers the message

1.7 Cooperation & Ethical Persuasion

Ethical persuasion should respect the rights, goals, and emotions of everyone involved. Building cooperation should never mean overriding people's needs just to get what you want at any cost.

Why don't people cooperate?

ReasonLooks likeHow to respond
Different goalsTheir objective genuinely differs from yours (or they think it does)Build agreement through logical reasoning
Indirect oppositionResistance with no clear reason or alternative offeredPersuasion based on sentiment / emotional appeal
Justifiable oppositionThey have a valid reason for not cooperatingDiscover the reason — you may need to be the one to adjust
Unjustified oppositionSelfish or unfair reasons; simply not interested in what you wantRecognize it for what it is before persuading

1.8 Four Interpersonal Communication Styles

StyleDefinitionExampleVerdict
AggressionForceful, rude, or disrespectful; wants to win even at others' expense"My idea is the best — if you don't like it, you don't understand anything!"Flawed
PassivityStays quiet, avoids conflict, lets others decide even when uncomfortableSaying nothing while a coworker takes credit for your workFlawed
ManipulativenessInfluences others secretly or unfairly to get what they wantFaking illness to dodge a project, then joining at the end to claim creditFlawed
AssertivenessExpresses thoughts, feelings, and needs clearly and respectfully"I understand your point, but I prefer we split the task equally."Healthiest — adopt this

Why the first three are flawed

  • Ineffective — they fail to solve problems or build relationships; people stop listening or responding well.
  • Stressful — they create tension for speaker and listener alike: arguments, misunderstandings, guilt.
  • Counterproductive — they make things worse: aggression pushes people away, manipulation breeds mistrust.
Assertive phrasing patterns worth memorizing: "I feel ___ when ___. Can we ___?" · "I respect your view, but I see it differently." · "I'm not comfortable with that, but I'm happy to find something that works for both of us."

1.9 The Seven C's of Effective Communication (Francis J. Bergin)

CMeaningExample
ClaritySimple, direct words so the message is easy to understand"I need your help with this task today."
ConcisenessShort and to the point; cut unnecessary detail/repetition—
ConcretenessSpecific facts or examples, not vague statements"The report needs sales data from January to March."
CorrectnessAccurate grammar, tone, and facts — builds credibility—
ConsiderationRespect the other person's feelings and viewpoint"I understand you're busy, but could we find a time that works for both of us?"
CompletenessThe message must stand alone — incomplete info forces another round of clarification—
CourtesyStay polite and positive even when raising a problem"Thanks for your effort — I'd like to suggest one small change."

These seven principles support assertive communication directly — together they reduce misunderstanding and build mutual respect.

1.10 Barriers to Communication

A barrier is anything that obstructs a message between sender and receiver, creating confusion, conflict, and bottlenecks. There are five recognized categories.

CategoryKey examples
1. Physical & MechanicalNoise, distance, time delays, information overload, outdated equipment, words with multiple meanings, denotation vs. connotation
2. Language / SemanticUnclear messages, words with multiple meanings, technical jargon, faulty translation, unclarified assumptions, mismatched body language
3. Socio-PsychologicalDiffering perception, differing attitudes, emotions, inattention, closed minds, premature evaluation, distrust, resistance to change, cultural differences
4. OrganizationalStatus relationships, one-way flow (no feedback), complex structure, rigid rules, distance, physical/mechanical defects
5. PersonalAttitude of superiors, lack of confidence in subordinates, insistence on "proper channel," ignoring communication, filtering information
Memory hook: Mechanical = the channel/equipment. Semantic = the words themselves. Socio-psychological = what's happening inside each person's head. Organizational = the structure they sit inside. Personal = how individual superiors/subordinates choose to behave.

1.11 The Communication Pyramid & The Pareto Principle

The Communication Pyramid shows that every message is filtered through a deep, hidden base — attitudes, beliefs, prejudices, assumptions, emotions, and memories — before it ever becomes the visible "tip": the actual words and actions a listener sees. The same message shown to a hundred people can land in a hundred subtly different ways, because the receiver often contributes up to 80% of a message's final meaning.

That 80% figure connects directly to the Pareto Principle (80/20 Rule) — originally an economic idea (80% of results come from 20% of effort), applied here to communication:

  • ~20% of your message (the key points) often drives ~80% of understanding
  • ~20% of your listening time may catch ~80% of what truly matters
  • ~20% of a team's communicators may produce ~80% of effective collaboration

How to apply it

  • Focus on key messages instead of overloading with detail
  • Spend more time crafting the main points of any email or presentation
  • Listen actively for the critical parts of a discussion
  • Fix the highest-frequency communication habits first (tone, clarity, body language)
Part 2 — Practice Questions
Q1

The Communication Process & The Seven C's

30 marks
At GreenLeaf Logistics, the operations manager, Priya, needed the warehouse team to switch to a new delivery schedule starting Monday. She typed a quick email late on Friday afternoon: "New schedule starts soon, please adjust." She did not specify a date, did not name which team members were affected, and sent it without checking it against the master roster. By Monday morning, half the warehouse staff showed up at the old shift times, deliveries were delayed, and customers complained. When Priya followed up by phone, two supervisors said they never saw the email because it was filtered into a low-priority folder by the company's email system.
a)Using the 10-step business communication process model, explain at which stage(s) Priya's communication broke down.05
b)Identify and explain three categories of barriers to communication present in this scenario, giving one specific example of each from the case.12
c)Using the Seven C's of Effective Communication, explain how Priya's message should have been composed to prevent this breakdown.13
Show Model Answer

a) Stage 2 ("defining the situation") was incomplete — Priya never worked out exactly who needed the message or by when. Stage 4 ("selecting a course of action") was rushed: she picked a low-richness channel (email) for an urgent, schedule-critical message. Stage 5 ("composing the message") was vague — no date, no names. On the receiving side, stage 7 ("receiving the message") failed outright for two supervisors because the message was filtered away before it ever reached them. The breakdown therefore spans both the sender's encoding and the channel/reception side of the process.

b) Language/Semantic — Unclear message: "starts soon" gives no date or names, so the receiver can't act correctly even if they read it. Physical/Mechanical — technical/equipment barrier: the email filter routed the message to a low-priority folder, breaking the channel itself for two supervisors. Organizational — one-way flow: Priya sent the message without requesting confirmation, so there was no feedback loop to catch the gap before Monday.

c) Clarity: state plainly — "Effective Monday, June 29, the warehouse team moves to the 7am–3pm shift." Conciseness: stick to who/what/when, no filler. Concreteness: name the exact date, shift times, and affected staff instead of "soon." Correctness: verify the date and team list against the master roster before sending. Consideration: acknowledge the short notice — "I know this is fast, let me know if it's a conflict." Completeness: include everything needed to act, so no follow-up question is required. Courtesy: close politely and invite questions. Given the urgency, Priya should also pair the email with a phone call or face-to-face check-in and request confirmation of receipt — closing the feedback gap identified in part (b).

Q2

Formal vs. Informal Networks & Ethical Persuasion

30 marks
TechNova Inc. is planning to merge two departments. Senior management has not yet made an official announcement, but a rumor starts circulating informally — at the coffee machine, in chat groups — that "half the team will be laid off." Within two days the rumor has reached almost everyone, productivity drops, and several employees start job-hunting. When management finally sends a formal memo explaining the merger will actually create new roles with no layoffs planned, many employees say they "already know the truth" and don't believe it.
a)Distinguish between the formal and informal communication networks at play in this scenario.05
b)Explain why the informal network (grapevine) outpaced and undermined the formal network here, referencing specific barriers to communication.12
c)As an HR communication strategist, using the principles of ethical persuasion for cooperation, recommend an approach to regain employees' trust.13
Show Model Answer

a) The formal network is the company's official channel — here, the memo eventually sent through established lines of authority. The informal network (the grapevine) is the unofficial, ever-shifting web of personal conversations — coffee-machine talk and chat groups — that follows no fixed pattern and carries no guarantee of accuracy.

b) Organizational — one-way flow/delay: management's silence created an information vacuum that employees filled themselves. Socio-Psychological — distrust and premature evaluation: once employees had already formed a belief via the grapevine, they judged the later formal memo through that lens and dismissed it rather than processing it with an open mind. Socio-Psychological — emotions: fear about job security colored how the rumor was interpreted and shared, accelerating its spread far beyond the calmer, slower official channel.

c) Ethical persuasion means respecting employees' rights, goals, and emotions rather than just pushing a message through. Management should: acknowledge the rumor directly and validate the anxiety it caused, rather than dismissing it; communicate earlier and more transparently in future to close the gap that let the grapevine take over; open two-way formal channels (Q&A sessions, anonymous question forms) so employees can raise justifiable concerns about job security and get them addressed individually; and avoid manipulative reassurance ("trust us") in favor of concrete facts about roles and timeline — the honest, sustainable route to rebuilding cooperation.

Q3

Interpersonal Communication Styles & Assertiveness

30 marks
On a product launch team, Farah regularly interrupts colleagues, saying things like "That's a weak idea — obviously we should do it my way." Nadia, who has strong design ideas, says nothing in meetings even when her work is criticized unfairly, because she "doesn't want to make a scene." Meanwhile, Omar privately tells Nadia he "really needs her help" finishing his part by Friday, implying he'll mention her contribution to the manager — but once she finishes the work, he presents it as entirely his own.
a)Identify the interpersonal communication style shown by Farah, Nadia, and Omar, with one piece of evidence for each.05
b)Using the ineffective/stressful/counterproductive framework, explain why all three approaches are likely to damage team performance over time.12
c)Writing as Nadia, draft two short assertive responses — one to Farah's criticism, one declining Omar's request — and explain why each is assertive rather than aggressive or passive.13
Show Model Answer

a) Farah — Aggression: she speaks forcefully and dismissively, prioritizing winning over respecting others. Nadia — Passivity: she stays silent even when criticized unfairly, letting others' views dominate to avoid conflict. Omar — Manipulativeness: he uses an implied reward (credit with the manager) to secure Nadia's free labor, then takes the credit himself — influencing her unfairly rather than asking directly.

b) Ineffective: Farah's aggression shuts down discussion instead of improving ideas; Nadia's silence means her genuinely strong ideas never reach the team; Omar's manipulation gets short-term task completion at the cost of an honest working relationship. Stressful: Farah's style creates defensiveness in meetings; Nadia accumulates frustration and resentment; Omar's deception, once discovered, creates guilt and anger. Counterproductive: over time Farah's aggression pushes teammates away from collaborating with her; Nadia's passivity teaches the team her boundaries can be ignored; Omar's manipulation destroys trust, making people less willing to cooperate with him again — the opposite of his goal.

c) To Farah: "I see this differently — I'd like to walk through my reasoning before we decide." This is assertive, not aggressive, because it states her position clearly and respectfully without insulting Farah's idea or backing down. To Omar: "I don't have time to take this on by Friday, but I'm happy to share notes so you can finish it yourself." This is assertive, not passive, because it states a clear boundary instead of silently agreeing, while still offering a reasonable alternative — mirroring the "sets a boundary but stays open" pattern from the assertive-communication examples.

Q4

Barriers to Communication & The Pareto Principle

30 marks
A global software team — two developers in Dhaka, one project manager in London, and a client liaison in Toronto — is collaborating on a project. Meetings run on a video tool that frequently freezes due to outdated office hardware in Dhaka. The London manager often uses jargon and idioms ("let's circle back once we've got the green light") that confuse the Dhaka developers. The Toronto liaison values strict scheduled punctuality, while the Dhaka team treats start times more flexibly, causing repeated frustration. After several missed deadlines, the team has simply stopped raising concerns.
a)Identify two physical/mechanical barriers present in this scenario.05
b)Explain how language/semantic barriers and socio-psychological barriers (including cultural differences) are each affecting this team, citing evidence.12
c)Using the Communication Pyramid and the Pareto Principle, recommend where the team should focus its limited effort first.13
Show Model Answer

a) Outdated video-conferencing hardware causing repeated freezing, and the geographic distance separating Dhaka, London, and Toronto, which limits opportunities for immediate clarification.

b) Language/Semantic: the London manager's jargon and idioms are a form of unclear, technical language that non-native English speakers may not interpret the same way, confusing the Dhaka developers. Socio-Psychological — cultural differences: the Toronto liaison's strict view of punctuality conflicts with the Dhaka team's more flexible attitude — a difference in attitude rooted in culture, generating recurring friction. Socio-Psychological — resistance/closed minds: after repeated missed deadlines, the team has stopped raising concerns altogether, reflecting growing inattention and quiet resistance — communication has effectively shut down because no one expects raising issues to help.

c) The Communication Pyramid shows the visible "tip" — words exchanged in meetings — is only a small part of what's really happening; the much larger hidden base is each person's assumptions about time, formality, and collaboration. The team's real problems sit beneath the surface, not in the words themselves. Applying the Pareto Principle, the team should target the 20% of root causes generating 80% of the friction: (1) the unreliable hardware, since it disrupts every single meeting, and (2) the unspoken cultural mismatch over punctuality and directness, since it strains every interaction. Fixing the equipment and explicitly agreeing on shared norms for jargon-free language and meeting-time expectations would resolve most of the team's friction, even if smaller issues remain.

Q5

Ethical Persuasion & Cooperation

30 marks
Hassan, a new team leader, wants his five-person sales team to switch from spreadsheets to a new CRM system. Three members agree right away. Layla resists strongly — the new system doesn't sync with a client database she relies on, so switching now would cause her to lose data. Faisal simply says, "I don't see why I should bother, the old way works fine for me," offers no further reason, and keeps using spreadsheets quietly even after the team agrees to switch.
a)Classify the type of opposition shown by Layla and the type shown by Faisal.05
b)Using the four reasons people don't cooperate, explain why each of the five team members is responding the way they are.12
c)As Hassan, describe an ethical persuasion strategy for Layla's resistance and for Faisal's resistance, respecting their rights, goals, and emotions.13
Show Model Answer

a) Layla shows justifiable opposition — she has a valid, specific reason (risk of data loss) for not switching yet. Faisal shows unjustified opposition bordering on indirect opposition — his refusal isn't backed by a real reason, and he continues to act against the team's agreed decision afterward.

b) The three members who agreed quickly likely share Hassan's goal of working more efficiently and saw the CRM as compatible with it — alignment of goals produced quick cooperation. Layla's concern about losing synced client data is legitimate, practical justifiable opposition; Hassan needs to treat it as valid information, not an obstacle to push past. Faisal's "the old way works fine for me" offers no concrete reason — unjustified opposition, possibly rooted in comfort with the status quo rather than any conflict with the team's actual goals.

c) For Layla: Hassan shouldn't use persuasion to "win" here — ethical persuasion means listening first. He should investigate the syncing issue with IT before asking her to switch, possibly delaying her transition until it's resolved, respecting her rights and goals instead of overriding a legitimate need. For Faisal: since there's no valid reason behind his resistance, Hassan can use logical reasoning and a self-interest appeal — showing concretely how the CRM reduces Faisal's own repetitive work — while staying clear and respectful that team-wide adoption is expected. This stays ethical because it persuades by connecting the change to a goal Faisal already has, rather than forcing compliance.
